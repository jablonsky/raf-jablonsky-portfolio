Optical Fibre Technology: From Waveguide Physics to Industrial Ethernet Design

Optical fibre is more than a faster replacement for copper: it is a physical transmission system whose optical behaviour, manufacturing precision and installation quality directly influence network reliability. This article modernises the original optical-fibre introduction and applies it to industrial Ethernet design.

Publication context: revised from the author's archived 12-page DOCS Hub article, 25 October 2026. Illustrations are reconstructed; operational figures and test values are identified separately. This page is a technical reference, not evidence of a completed OT fibre installation.

1. Why optical fibre is useful in networking

The original article identifies bandwidth, long-distance transmission, electromagnetic-interference immunity, scalability, reliability, security and potential long-term cost benefits. These remain useful design drivers, especially between buildings, industrial cabinets and electrically noisy machinery areas.

2. Fibre construction: core, cladding and coating

The article's manufacturing section explains how a higher-refractive-index glass core is surrounded by lower-index cladding, forming an optical waveguide. Protective coating is distinct from the optical cladding and is essential for handling and mechanical protection. Typical telecom cladding is around 125 µm; primary coatings commonly bring the coated fibre diameter to approximately 250 µm, with subsequent buffers and cable jackets added according to the product design.

3. Light and wavelength

The original article introduces the relationship between wavelength and frequency using c = λf for light travelling in vacuum. Inside glass, propagation speed is reduced and is more precisely related to the medium's refractive index and group velocity. Networking optics commonly operate at wavelengths such as 850 nm, 1310 nm and 1550 nm.

Nominal wavelengthCommon networking contextEngineering reminder
850 nmMany short-reach multimode Ethernet optical links.Check OM grade and exact transceiver reach.
1310 nmCommon single-mode Ethernet optics and some other optical systems.A wavelength alone does not determine link reach.
1550 nmLonger-reach single-mode optics and many WDM systems.Consider attenuation, dispersion, amplification and equipment compatibility.

The visible-light colour table in the legacy article is a useful optics introduction, but Ethernet fibre wavelengths are generally in the infrared; visible colour names do not describe the operating light of these links. The original claim of 7,000 km transmission is not a reach specification for passive fibre alone: transoceanic systems depend on repeaters or optical amplification and specialised design.

4. Guiding light: total internal reflection and waveguide modes

The original article uses total internal reflection to explain light guidance. This is a helpful geometric model for a core with higher refractive index than the cladding, but a complete description relies on electromagnetic waveguide modes. Modes explain why multimode fibre behaves differently from single-mode fibre.

With multimode fibre, different supported modes can have different propagation delays, creating modal dispersion. Graded-index multimode fibre reduces this effect. Single-mode fibre supports only one spatial mode under appropriate operating conditions and therefore avoids intermodal dispersion, although chromatic dispersion and other impairments can still matter.

The original article compares OM1 through OM5 and OS1/OS2. Those categories should be retained, but decisions require the exact Ethernet transceiver specification rather than generic claims that a given fibre type always reaches a set distance.

6. How fibre is manufactured

A distinctive strength of the original article is its manufacturing explanation. It discusses Outside Vapour Deposition (OVD) and Modified Chemical Vapour Deposition (MCVD) to produce a controlled glass preform. The preform is heated and drawn to fibre diameter, then coated, cured, inspected and wound onto reels. Core chemistry and precision affect refractive-index profile and optical characteristics.

For a manufacturing engineer, this is a practical link between process capability and network performance: dimensional control, material purity, surface defects, coating quality and handling determine whether the physical product meets its optical and mechanical requirements.

7. Fibre selection for industrial Ethernet

In an OT installation, the specification should begin with the application and environment—not with a preference for fibre over copper. Consider required data rate, span length, switch/SFP availability, electromagnetic environment, route separation, temperature ratings, vibration, chemical exposure, mechanical protection, connectors and maintenance strategy.

Design questionWhat to record
What are the endpoints?Exact switch and SFP models, connector types, approved optics and fibre standards
What is the environment?Indoor/outdoor, cabinet and cable ratings, temperature, vibration, chemicals and mechanical risk
How much optical loss?Length, attenuation per km, connector/splice losses and engineering reserve
How is resilience implemented?Diverse cable routes, switch redundancy and tested reconvergence, not just dual strands
How is it maintained?Label scheme, as-built records, optical measurements, spare modules and change control

8. Link budget: the engineering calculation missing from the first edition

The practical maximum distance is constrained by the optics and total link loss. For a purely illustrative design, take a transmitter minimum power of −5 dBm, receiver sensitivity of −18 dBm, 5 km of fibre at 0.35 dB/km, four connector interfaces at 0.5 dB each and a 3 dB reserve.

Do not use these numbers as a design approval. Real design requires transceiver minimum/maximum power, receiver sensitivity and overload limits, wavelength-dependent losses, dispersion and compatible optics. Use appropriate loss measurements and vendor specifications.

9. Commissioning and troubleshooting

Before commissioning: inspect and clean connector end-faces, verify compatible transceivers, fibre type, TX/RX polarity, route length and labelling. Measure optical loss with suitable test equipment where required; review switch diagnostics and errors. An optical time-domain reflectometer (OTDR) can help locate joints and discontinuities where appropriate, but it is not a substitute for all insertion-loss acceptance tests.

10. OT design significance

Fibre can provide useful electrical separation across industrial infrastructure and support greater separation between cabinets or plant areas. It is not inherently a cybersecurity boundary: VLANs, routing, firewalls and secure management are still required. Similarly, fibre immunity to EMI does not prove the overall OT network will be available during a power, optics, switch or cable-route failure.

For a Purdue-oriented network, document each physical fibre connection as part of its network conduit: endpoints, permitted logical traffic, maintenance ownership and failover behaviour. This connects physical design decisions with segmentation, risk assessment and fault recovery.

Engineering conclusion

Choosing optical fibre is a system engineering decision. The original article explains the physics and manufacturing; the updated version connects those principles to actual questions of Ethernet optics compatibility, link loss, installation quality and industrial availability.
